Eligibility check

Two to five clicks. No email, no account, and nothing leaves your browser. If a word here is unfamiliar, tap what do these mean? — no question is a silly one.

Which of these sounds like you?

Building a company — you're starting a business, or already run one. Even if it's just you and an idea right now, pick this.

Public code project — you wrote code that you put on the internet for anyone to use and copy, usually on GitHub. This is what "open source" means. Companies give these away free because free tools make their platform popular.

Where does your money come from?

Just an idea so far — you haven't registered a company yet, or you registered it but there's no website or product. Completely normal. Pick this and we'll show you the short list of things to put in place first.

My own money — the company exists and you pay for everything yourself, from savings or from what customers pay you. The industry word for this is bootstrapped. Most founders in the world are here.

I'm in an accelerator — you got into a programme like Y Combinator, Techstars or a local equivalent. They give you a small amount of money, advice and contacts over about three months, in exchange for a small share of your company.

Investors put money in — a venture capital firm (a "VC") or a wealthy individual (an "angel") gave you money in exchange for owning part of your company. This unlocks the biggest credit amounts, because the cloud companies trust the investor's judgement.

How old is the company?
Roughly how much money does it make a year?

This means total money customers paid you over the last year, before costs. If you're not selling anything yet, that's $0 — pick Under $1M. Nearly every programme here is aimed at companies under $1M, so this rarely counts against you.

How popular is your project?

On GitHub, a star is a bookmark — anyone who likes your project can click a star on it. The number is public and sits at the top of your project page. It's the rough measure companies use to judge how popular a project is.

Under 100 is completely fine. Plenty of the tools here ask only that your code is public.

What licence is on it?

A licence is a short text file in your project saying what other people are allowed to do with your code. Without one, legally nobody may use it.

Most companies here require a standard, recognised licence — MIT and Apache 2.0 are the common ones, and MIT is the simplest. Adding one takes about a minute on GitHub, and it unlocks roughly a third more programmes on this page.

You're earlier than most of these programmes expect — here's the fix

Almost every company on this page asks for the same five basic things before they'll give you anything. None of them need funding, permission, or much money. Once these exist, most of the list opens up.

  1. Register the company. Nearly every programme requires a real, registered business — not a person. Whatever's normal and cheapest where you live is fine.
  2. Buy a domain name and put your email on it. This is the one that quietly disqualifies the most people. Cloudflare and DigitalOcean both refuse applications from gmail.com addresses — they want you@yourcompany.com. A domain costs about $10 a year.
  3. Put up a website, even a single page. It has to be publicly reachable and it has to load. It can say what you're building and nothing else. Reviewers open it before they approve you.
  4. Create a LinkedIn, X or GitHub page for the company. Cloudflare asks for this by name. It's a five-minute job and it proves you're real.
  5. Build something that works, however rough. The industry calls it an MVP — minimum viable product, the smallest version that does the main thing. IBM and Oracle both require one.
